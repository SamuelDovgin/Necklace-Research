# 08 - 14K vs 18K and Yellow vs White Gold

Compiled: 2026-07-24

## Bottom line for this necklace

**14K yellow gold is the practical default.** It preserves the original warm, unplated appearance, contains enough alloying metal for a generally more wear-resistant delicate chain, and costs less than an equivalent 18K build. Choose 18K yellow gold if the richer gold color and higher gold content matter more than price and modestly greater susceptibility to scratching or deformation. [1][2]

Choose white gold only for the cool white appearance. The StarsGem white-gold listings are rhodium plated, so their bright white finish introduces a maintenance item that the unplated yellow-gold reference does not have.

## 14K vs 18K

| Consideration | 14K gold | 18K gold |
|---|---|---|
| Gold content | About 58.3%; commonly marked 585 | 75%; commonly marked 750 |
| Alloy content | About 41.7% other metals | 25% other metals |
| Typical wear tradeoff | Generally harder and more resistant to deformation, but exact alloy and manufacture matter | Generally softer and easier to scratch or deform, but exact alloy and manufacture matter |
| Yellow color | Lighter/less saturated | Richer, deeper yellow |
| Cost | Lower gold-material cost | Higher gold-material cost |
| Skin sensitivity | More alloying metal, so the exact nickel/copper/palladium recipe matters more | More gold and less alloying metal, but still not automatically hypoallergenic |
| Best use here | Fine daily-wear chain and value-focused build | Premium color/feel when budget and careful wear are acceptable |

The FTC explains that 14K is 14 parts gold plus 10 parts other metal, while 18K is 18 parts gold plus 6 parts other metal. [2] Do not compare quotes using grams alone: identical-looking 14K and 18K pieces can use different dimensions, alloy recipes, clasps, solder, and stone weights.

## Yellow vs white gold

| Consideration | Yellow gold | White gold |
|---|---|---|
| Color | Warm gold; 18K is usually more saturated than 14K | Cool gray-white alloy, commonly brightened with rhodium |
| Common alloying metals | Often silver, copper, and zinc | May include nickel or palladium plus copper/zinc |
| Plating | Usually unnecessary when ordered unplated | Often rhodium plated for a brighter white surface |
| Maintenance | Normal cleaning and inspection; no white plating to renew | Rhodium gradually wears and may require occasional replating |
| Allergy question | Ask for the full alloy if sensitive to copper or other metals | Specifically ask whether it is nickel-free or palladium-based |
| Visual effect with DEF diamonds | Warm contrast around colorless stones | Cooler, lower-contrast “white-on-white” presentation |
| Best use here | Matches the original design and avoids plating maintenance | Use only if the white-metal look is preferred enough to accept replating and alloy questions |

GIA notes that white gold is made by alloying gold with white metals such as nickel or palladium and is often rhodium plated. GIA also warns nickel-sensitive buyers to avoid nickel-alloyed white gold and notes that rhodium wears gradually and requires occasional replating. [3][4]

## Recommendation by priority

- **Best balance:** 14K yellow gold, unplated, stamped 585.
- **Richer luxury version:** 18K yellow gold, unplated, stamped 750; keep the same minimum chain dimensions and finished-weight requirement.
- **Cool white appearance:** 14K or 18K white gold only after confirming the underlying alloy, nickel content, rhodium plating, and future replating terms.
- **Durability priority:** chain width, thickness, link geometry, clasp, solder quality, and guaranteed finished weight matter more than karat alone.

For the StarsGem comparison, ask for the heavier **AuDP4011413(p1)** architecture in both 14K and 18K **yellow** gold. This separates the construction upgrade from the white-gold/rhodium choice.

## Supplier questions

1. Is the piece composed throughout of solid 14K/585 or 18K/750 gold, and is any part hollow?
2. What is the exact alloy recipe? For white gold, is it nickel-based or palladium-based?
3. Is the piece rhodium plated? If yes, what is the plating specification and what does replating cost?
4. What are the guaranteed finished weight, chain-only weight, width, thickness, link dimensions, clasp, and pendant weight for each karat/color option?
5. Will changing from 18K white to 14K yellow alter the CAD dimensions, chain gauge, gross weight, price, or production time?
6. Can the invoice state “unplated solid 14K yellow gold, 585” or the exact alternative selected?

## Evidence gaps and cautions

- Hardness, color, density, and allergy risk depend on the supplier's actual alloy recipe and manufacturing process; karat and color names alone do not fully specify performance.
- A 585 or 750 stamp is useful evidence but should still match the invoice and final QC. For a high-value order, ask whether an independent metal test is available.
- “Solid gold” can be used ambiguously in ordinary conversation. The FTC consumer guidance defines it as not hollow; the order should separately state karat, construction, and absence of plating/filling. [2]
- Rhodium-maintenance intervals vary with plating thickness, wear, skin chemistry, and care; do not accept a universal promised interval without a supplier warranty.
